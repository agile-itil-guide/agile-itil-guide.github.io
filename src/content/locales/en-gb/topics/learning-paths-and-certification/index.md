# Learning Paths and Certification

**People learn this subject best by following a path that fits their role and their work, and certifications can help along the way, but they show that someone studied a framework and not that they run services well.**

## Why this matters

A guide with 49 topics can overwhelm a new reader. A service desk analyst does not need to start with value stream management, and a product owner does not need to start with configuration management databases. Reading in a sensible order, for your role, saves time and builds understanding in the right sequence.

Certification raises separate questions. Employers ask for it, training providers sell it, and individuals wonder whether it is worth the cost and effort. Some certifications are for individuals only, some are for organisations, and some are both. Marketing often blurs the difference. A team that wants to learn needs an honest picture: what each scheme is for, who runs it, what it does and does not show, and where free or low-cost alternatives exist.

This topic offers learning paths through this guide by role, and a vendor-neutral map of the main certification schemes that the sources read for this guide describe. It does not endorse any scheme or provider. Prices, exam formats, and catalogs change often, and the sources read here give few of them, so check each owner's current page before deciding. Some of the pages needed were not reachable when this topic was written; where that limits a statement, the text says so.

### Learning by doing

Reading is the start. The guide is designed for use with a team: each topic has questions to discuss, a worked example, and prompts and skills. A simple learning rhythm is to read one topic, hold a 45-minute conversation using its questions (see Topic 6.1 — Team Conversations), choose one small change, and review it a month later. That follows the agile learning idea of short cycles of learning and doing (see Topic 1.10 — Agile Learning).

### The certification landscape

The sources read for this guide describe the following schemes. The table summarises what they say and, importantly, what they do not.

| Area | Scheme or body | What the sources say |
|---|---|---|
| **ITIL** | PeopleCert (owner of ITIL since 2021) | Wikipedia: certification is only for individuals, not organisations; the ITIL 4 path starts with Foundation and branches into Managing Professional (four specialist modules) and Strategic Leader (two). PeopleCert's page: the catalog can be filtered by ITIL 4 or ITIL (Version 5), with paths named Managing Professional, Strategic Leader, Practice Manager, and Extension Modules, and an upgrade route for ITIL 4 certified professionals to ITIL Foundation (Version 5) |
| **ISO/IEC 20000** | Certification bodies for organisations; training bodies for individuals | Wikipedia: organisations can be certified, and individuals can earn qualifications from bodies such as APMG-International, EXIN, and PECB |
| **ISO/IEC 27001** | Independent certification bodies | Wikipedia: ISO does not certify organisations; independent bodies do, in stages with surveillance audits and recertification on a three-year cycle (see Topic 3.13 — Information Security Management) |
| **Scrum** | Scrum Alliance (founded 2002) and Scrum.org (founded after Ken Schwaber left the Alliance in late 2009) | Wikipedia: the Alliance oversees the "Certified Scrum" accreditation series, and Scrum.org oversees the "Professional Scrum" series; the article names no credentials, costs, or exam rules |
| **SAFe** | Scaled Agile, Inc. | Wikipedia: an academy and accreditation scheme, with certifications covering different areas and knowledge levels; no names or details given |
| **COBIT** | ISACA | Wikipedia: ISACA offers certification on COBIT 2019 and COBIT 5 |
| **FinOps** | FinOps Foundation | The Foundation's page: training and certification, including FinOps Certified Practitioner and Professional credentials |
| **FitSM** | FitSM project | Wikipedia: organisations cannot be certified as conformant, though conformance can be audited; a personnel certification scheme began in 2013 |
| **DevOps, SRE, Kanban** | Several providers | The sources read name no single certifying body for DevOps or SRE; the Kanban Guide's page was read for its content, not for any certification. Check providers' own pages and look for independence |

Details such as exam formats, pass marks, prices, and renewal rules are not in the sources read and are deliberately left out. They change, and the owners' pages are the place to look.

### What a certificate shows

A certificate typically shows that a person studied a body of knowledge and passed an assessment. It can be a useful sign of shared vocabulary and effort. It does not show that someone can run an incident well or design a good process. The Wikipedia article on ITIL says there is no formal independent third-party assessment of an organisation's ITIL compliance; for organisations, ISO/IEC standards provide certifiable schemes. A certificate for a person is not a certificate for a team.

### Choosing

Questions to ask before paying for a course or exam:

- **What problem will it help me or my team solve?** Name the task you will do better.
- **Who runs the scheme, and is it independent of the provider selling the course?** Owners accredit training providers in many schemes.
- **What does the scheme claim, and what is the evidence?** The sources read offer none on whether certificates improve outcomes.
- **Is the content current?** Check the version, as with ITIL 4 and ITIL Version 5 (see Topic 2.2 — ITIL).
- **What does it cost in money and time, including renewal?** Check the owner's page.
- **Are there free sources that cover the same ground?** Many frameworks publish guides freely, such as the Scrum Guide and the Kanban Guide, and this guide itself is free.
- **Will my employer or customers value it?** Ask them what they need.

### Alternatives and complements

Certificates are one route. Others: reading the primary sources named in each topic, communities of practice, pairing with experienced colleagues, internal workshops, book clubs, conference talks, and short experiments at work (see Topic 3.9 — Knowledge Management). For teams, a skills map and shared learning goals often do more than individual certificates (see Topic 1.10 — Agile Learning).

### Learning with AI

AI assistants can help learners: explaining a concept, generating practice questions, role-playing a stakeholder, or critiquing a draft. They can also invent facts, so check explanations against the primary source (see Topic 6.2 — AI in IT Service Management and Topic 6.3 — AI Skills and Prompts). Do not use an assistant to complete an assessment where the rules forbid it.

### Limits of the sources

The Wikipedia articles and owner pages read for this guide describe schemes briefly, and several pages were unavailable when this topic was written. They give no evidence of the effect of certification on outcomes. No endorsement of any scheme or provider is intended or implied. ## Best practices

1. **Start from a work problem.** Choose what to learn by what you need to do better at work this quarter, then choose a path or course.
2. **Follow a role path.** Use the table above as a starting order, adjust it, and share it with a colleague who has a different role.
3. **Learn with your team.** Pair reading with a short team conversation, so that learning becomes change. Hold one a month.
4. **Read the primary sources.** Where a framework is free to read, such as the Scrum Guide and the Kanban Guide, start there before any course.
5. **Check the owner's current page.** Before enrolling, verify the scheme's current version, content, and requirements on the owner's own site.
6. **Ask what the certificate is for.** Be clear whether you need personal knowledge, a signal to employers, or an organisational certificate; they are different.
7. **Check independence.** Prefer providers that are accredited by the scheme's owner, and be careful of claims that a course guarantees outcomes.
8. **Mix routes.** Combine reading, practice, mentoring, and, if useful, a certificate. Do not rely on one.
9. **Practice soon.** Apply what you learn within a week, however small, and tell your team. Learning that is not used fades.
10. **Keep a team skills map.** List the skills the services need and who has them, and plan learning to fill the gaps (see the training needs analysis skill).
11. **Budget time and money.** Include study time in plans and treat it as work, not as something done in free evenings.
12. **Review value.** After a year, ask what changed because of the learning, and adjust your approach to certification accordingly.

## Questions to discuss with your team

**What skills does our service need that we do not have, and who could learn them?**

Draw up a simple map of skills against people. Look for single points of expertise and for gaps. Ask what learning route fits each gap: reading, pairing, a course, a certificate. Choose one gap to close this quarter.

**What do we hope a certificate will do for us, and is there evidence it does?**

Collect the reasons: a requirement from a customer, a signal to employers, a shared vocabulary, personal development. Discuss which are met by a certificate and which by something else. The facilitator can note that the sources read for this guide offer no evidence on outcomes. Agree on what you will measure if you invest.

**Which of our people have certificates, and what do they actually use?**

Ask holders what they use in daily work. Often the shared vocabulary is the main benefit. Discuss how to spread what they know. Plan a short session where holders teach colleagues.

**What are we required to have by customers or regulators, and where does that requirement come from?**

Find the text of any requirement for certification. Confirm whether it is for people or for the organisation, and which scheme. Many assumptions turn out to be wrong. Agree a note that records the source.

**How do we help new joiners learn quickly?**

Ask recent joiners what helped and what did not. Typical answers: a buddy, a short reading list, access to real work early. Draft a first-month path using the table above. Review it with the next joiner.

**How would we know that our learning is paying off?**

Ask what would change in the work: faster onboarding, fewer escalations, better incident reviews. Choose one signal and watch it for a quarter. The aim is to connect learning to results, as in Topic 5.6 — Metrics and Measurement.

## In practice: an agile ITIL example

A national statistics office's IT department has 120 staff. Over two years it has trained 40 people in ITIL 4 Foundation, paid for by a central budget, and the head of IT asks whether it has made a difference. Service desk staff say they now use the same words as colleagues, but delivery teams say the courses have not changed how they work. A customer survey shows no change in satisfaction. The training budget is under review.

The head of IT asks a small group to design something more useful. They begin with a skills map for the department's main services, built from a short survey and the incident history. It shows that few people understand incident command, that two engineers hold most of the knowledge of the main data pipeline, and that nobody on the delivery teams has read the Kanban Guide or the Scrum Guide, though two teams say they use them. The group proposes a role-based path: service desk staff and new joiners start with the first topics of this guide, delivery teams read the two guides and practice together, and engineers pair on the pipeline. Each month, one topic is chosen and a 45-minute conversation is held using its questions. A few people take a certificate where their work calls for it, and the budget favours those who will teach colleagues afterwards.

After a year, new joiners reach independent work faster by the managers' account, three more engineers can support the pipeline, and the department's incident reviews improve. Some difficulties remain. The monthly conversations lapse when workload peaks, a few staff feel the move from certificates to paths devalues their credentials, and a customer contract still asks for ITIL-certified staff. The head of IT sets a rule that a conversation is never canceled but may be shortened, recognises certificate holders as teachers in the program, and asks procurement to check whether the contract wording refers to individuals or to the organisation.

## Four perspectives

### Product and delivery teams

Delivery teams can learn most from practice: pair with operations, read the primary guides for the frameworks you use, and bring questions from this guide to your retrospectives. Take a certificate when it serves a task you will do soon.

### Service and operations teams

Operations teams carry much of the vocabulary and knowledge of service management. Share it through short sessions and onboarding paths. Use certificates where employers or customers need them, and teach what you learn.

### Leadership and governance

Leaders decide how much to invest in learning. Ask what changes in the work because of learning, support time and paths, and be clear about what certification does and does not show. Check whether requirements concern individuals or the organisation.

### Customers and users

Customers and users may ask for certified staff or providers. Ask what you need: a certified management system, trained individuals, or evidence of good practice. A clear question helps providers answer honestly.

## Common failure modes

- **Certificates as the goal.** Effort goes into passing exams, not into better work. Instead, tie learning to problems and measure what changes.
- **Confusing individual and organisational certification.** Claims go beyond what a scheme shows. Instead, state exactly what is certified and by whom.
- **No application.** People learn and return to old habits. Instead, practice within a week and hold team conversations.
- **One-size paths.** Everyone reads the same material. Instead, follow role-based paths and adapt them.
- **Buying without checking.** Courses are chosen on marketing. Instead, check the owner's current page and the provider's accreditation.
- **Ignoring free sources.** Money goes on courses while primary guides sit unread. Instead, begin with the free primary sources.
- **Learning in isolation.** Knowledge stays with individuals. Instead, share it through teaching and communities of practice.
- **No review.** Nobody asks if the learning paid off. Instead, choose a signal and review after a year.

## Maturity model

| Dimension | Initiate | Develop | Standardize | Manage | Orchestrate |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Role-based paths | Learning is individual and ad hoc | Some teams suggest reading | Role paths and onboarding paths exist and are used | Path use and results are reviewed | Paths are shared and improved across teams, partners, and customers |
| Use of certification | Certificates are collected without a plan | Some roles have goals for certification | A policy says when certificates help and what each shows | Certification value is reviewed against outcomes | Certification choices are coordinated with customers and suppliers |
| Learning in teams | Learning stays with individuals | Some teams hold learning sessions | Regular team conversations apply learning to real work | Learning effects are measured | Learning spreads across teams continuously |
| Skills visibility | Skills are unknown | Some skills lists exist | A skills map for each service is kept current | Gaps and learning plans are tracked | The skills picture spans the organisation and its suppliers |

## AI prompts

**Purpose: build a learning path for a role.**

```text
Help me build a learning path through this guide for the role of [role] in a team that [describe the team's work].

The topics available (paste the list of titles from the table of contents): [paste].
What the person already knows: [describe].
Time available per week: [hours].
The three biggest problems their work faces: [list].

Propose a four-to-eight-week path: topics in order, one conversation or activity per week, and one small change to try at work. Use only the topics I listed. Explain in a sentence why each topic is included. Ask clarifying questions first, and list what you are unsure of.
```

**Purpose: compare certification options for a stated need.**

```text
I need to decide whether to pursue a certificate. My need: [describe the work problem or requirement].
Options I am considering, with the owner's current description copied from their own page: [paste text for each; do not rely on memory].
Requirements from customers or regulators, with source text: [paste, or write "none known"].

Using only the pasted text, make a table of: who runs each scheme, whether it is for individuals or organisations, what it says it shows, what it does not say, and questions I should ask the owner. Do not state prices, pass marks, or formats that are not in the text. List what you are unsure of.
```

**Purpose: create a skills map from your services and people.**

```text
Help me create a skills map for [team].

Services and the skills each needs (my list): [paste].
People and the skills they have, as best I know (use roles, not names, if you prefer): [paste].

Produce a table with skills as rows and people as columns. Mark who has each skill, and highlight skills held by only one person or nobody. For each highlighted skill, suggest one low-cost way to learn it. Use only my lists. Ask clarifying questions first, and list what you are unsure of.
```

**Purpose: plan a monthly learning conversation series.**

```text
Plan a six-month series of 45-minute monthly learning conversations for a team of [number] people who [describe work].

Topics we could use from this guide (paste the list of topic titles you are interested in): [paste].
Current frustrations: [list].

Give a schedule that links each month's topic to a current frustration, a format for each session (for example lean coffee, discussion of questions, or a short exercise), a way to choose and track one small change, and a check at month six. Keep it tool-free. Ask clarifying questions first, mark assumptions, and list what you are unsure of.
```
