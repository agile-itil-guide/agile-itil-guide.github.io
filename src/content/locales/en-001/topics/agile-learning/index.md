# Agile Learning

**Agile learning applies short cycles, real work, and regular reflection to how people learn, and for service teams it is a practical way to build skills quickly while recognizing that some training, especially for safety and security, cannot be left to self-direction.**

## Why this matters

IT service teams face a steady flow of new tools, new threats, and new ways of working. Few organizations can afford to take people away for weeks of classroom training every time the ground shifts. Meanwhile, much of what a good engineer or service desk analyst knows was learned on the job, from incidents, from colleagues, and from trying things. Agile learning is a deliberate version of that: learning organized in short cycles around real tasks, with feedback and reflection built in.

The idea also fits the way agile teams already work. A team that holds retrospectives is already practicing a form of agile learning, and a team that treats each incident as a chance to learn is applying it to operations. What the term adds is a way to plan and support learning with the same discipline as delivery: a goal, a short cycle, a result that others can see, and a review.

The evidence base is modest. The [Wikipedia article on agile learning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agile_learning) carries maintenance notices about citations and tone, and much of its evidence comes from a small number of German-language sources and conference papers. Treat this topic as a set of well-motivated practices to test in your own team, not as a proven method.

## Core concepts

### What agile learning is

The Wikipedia article defines agile learning as the application of agile project methods, especially Scrum, to learning. Learning proceeds in incremental steps through iterative cycles that alternate between learning and doing, and tutors act as supporters rather than primary instructors. The article notes a narrower sense of the term as well: competence-oriented, media-based learning inside companies' work processes. It is also used in e-learning and online settings.

The method adapts Scrum, the time-boxed framework described in Topic 1.4 — Delivery Forecasting and Planning Metrics, to training. The article cites studies such as Masullo and colleagues (2005) on agile e-learning for manufacturing, Hansen and colleagues (2009) on agile learning and cooperation, and Longmuß, Höhne, and colleagues (2016–2017) on workplace learning for vocationally trained experts.

### Key ideas

The article lists ideas that recur in agile learning:

- **Break learning into manageable phases.** A large learning goal becomes a series of short cycles.
- **Turn shared goals into personal goals.** A team objective is divided so that each person has something specific to learn.
- **Base tasks on real work.** Learning tasks come from the actual working context.
- **Use cycles with visible results.** Each cycle ends with something that is presented and checked.
- **Apply transparency, verification, and adaptation.** These are the pillars of Scrum, applied to learning.
- **Build "learning agility."** The ability to take feedback and apply it to other tasks.
- **Allow safe mistakes.** The article describes a "soft failure environment" in which errors carry low risk.

### Three roles

For workplaces the article proposes three roles, borrowed from Scrum:

| Role | Scrum equivalent | What it does for learning |
|---|---|---|
| **Sponsor** | Product Owner | Defines the learning field, creates the organizational framework, and receives progress reports |
| **Coach** | Scrum Master | Provides technical, didactic, and methodical support and guides reflection |
| **Team** | Team | Peers with varied backgrounds pursue personal goals, work together (often digitally), and reflect regularly |

### Agile learning in education

The article describes students acting as the "client" of their own learning, with the learning process taking the place of the software development process and increments showing growing ability. It mentions projects anchored in real problems or industry clients, and cites a study by Noguera and colleagues (2018) suggesting that agile strategies in online higher education supported team regulation and project management. It also cites Vellasamy (2024) on applying Scrum retrospectives to educator professional development. These findings come from specific settings and may not carry over to yours.

The article lists related ideas, including problem-based learning, project-based learning, and mastery learning. Each has its own literature, and the choice among them depends on the learners and the subject.

### Limits the article states

The article is clear about limits. It says agile learning cannot reliably cover a predefined curriculum, because learning is driven by individual goals. For subjects where completeness matters, such as workplace safety or fire protection, it says classical training is preferred and agile learning can only supplement it. The same logic applies to IT: security awareness, safety procedures, and regulatory training need defined content and a record that everyone completed it.

### Learning in the service organization

ITIL treats knowledge and people as part of service management. Its practices such as knowledge management, continual improvement, and organizational change management all depend on people learning (see Topic 3.9 — Knowledge Management and Topic 3.15 — Continual Improvement). Agile learning gives a way to organize that learning inside teams, with the following common forms:

| Form | What it looks like | Where it fits |
|---|---|---|
| **Learning sprints** | A team sets a learning goal for a short cycle, such as a new monitoring tool, and shows what it can now do | Adopting a tool or technique |
| **Pairing and shadowing** | A learner works beside an experienced colleague on real tasks | Onboarding, on-call preparation |
| **Post-incident learning** | The team reviews an incident, records what it learned, and shares it | Operations and problem management |
| **Communities of practice** | People across teams meet regularly to share what works | Spreading practices across teams |
| **Retrospectives** | The team reflects on how it works and picks one change | Team-level improvement |
| **Formal training** | Defined courses with assessed outcomes | Safety, security, regulation, certifications |

### Measuring learning

Measuring learning is hard. Completion rates and course counts show activity, not capability. Better signals come from the work: whether a person can handle a task they could not before, how long onboarding takes, and whether incident handling improves. Use such signals to start conversations, and avoid treating them as scores for individuals.

## Best practices

1. **Start from real work.** Choose learning goals that come from tasks the team does or will do soon. Learning that is tied to a task is applied quickly and remembered longer than learning without a use.
2. **Plan learning in short cycles.** Give a learning goal one to four weeks, a visible result, and a short review. Treat the cycle length as something to adjust, just as you would for delivery.
3. **Make a named person responsible for learning priorities.** Following the article's sponsor role, choose someone who decides which skills matter most and can free time for them. Without this, learning loses out to delivery.
4. **Provide a coach.** A coach helps people reflect and find resources, and keeps learning from being treated as a side activity. The coach can be a team lead, an experienced colleague, or an outside facilitator.
5. **Turn team goals into personal goals.** Ask each person what they want to be able to do in three months, and connect it to the team's needs. This makes learning individual and relevant.
6. **Build safe places to fail.** Use test environments, game days, and low-risk tasks for practice. Tell people explicitly that mistakes in these settings are expected and that they will not be blamed.
7. **Show what you learned.** End each cycle with a short demonstration or write-up that others can use. This spreads knowledge and turns private learning into a shared asset (see Topic 3.9 — Knowledge Management).
8. **Learn from incidents on purpose.** After each significant incident, record what was learned and who needs to know. Treat the review as a learning cycle, not only as a search for causes (see Topic 3.2 — Incident Management).
9. **Keep formal training where it is required.** Use defined courses for safety, security, and regulated topics, and keep records of completion. Use agile learning as a supplement for applying and practicing what was taught.
10. **Reserve time.** Set aside a regular share of working time for learning, and protect it from delivery pressure. Treat a team that never has time to learn as a team that will run out of skills.
11. **Support multiple routes.** People learn through pairing, reading, courses, experiments, and teaching others. Offer a mix, and let people choose what suits them.
12. **Review whether learning is working.** Once a quarter, ask what the team can do now that it could not do before, and what is still missing. Use the answer to set the next learning goals.

## Questions to discuss with your team

**What did each of us learn last quarter, and how did we learn it?**

Ask people to name one thing and the route: a course, a colleague, an incident, an experiment. Collect the answers on a wall. Teams usually find that most learning came from work and people, not formal courses. Ask what made those routes work, and how to make more of them possible. The follow-up is whether any learning stayed with one person.

**What skills would we be in trouble without, and who has them?**

List the skills the service depends on and mark who holds each. Skills held by one person are risks. The aim is not blame but a plan for sharing. Ask which of these skills could be taught by pairing within a month. Choose one to start with.

**How do we learn from incidents today, and how do others find out what we learned?**

Look at the last three incidents and ask what was written down, who saw it, and what changed. Common answers are that the review stayed with the team, or that actions were assigned and not completed. Ask how a new colleague would find out about past incidents. Agree on one improvement to the way you share lessons.

**What training are we required to do, and what do we need beyond it?**

List the compulsory courses, such as security awareness, and ask whether people find them useful. Then list the skills the team needs that no course covers. The Wikipedia article makes clear that defined training is preferred where completeness matters. The discussion is about the balance, and whether the required training could be paired with practice on real tasks.

**Do we feel safe making mistakes while learning, and where do we not?**

Ask people to describe a time they hesitated to try something because of the risk of failure. Ask what would make it safer, such as a test environment, a pair, or agreement about blame. Listen for places where fear is rational, such as production systems, and look for safer ways to practice. The facilitator should keep the focus on conditions and not on individuals.

**How do we know if learning is making us better at our work?**

Ask what changes in the work would show that learning has paid off: faster onboarding, fewer escalations, smoother changes. Avoid measures of attendance. Discuss what data the team already has. Pick one signal to watch for a quarter and agree how to discuss it.

## In practice: an agile ITIL example

A regional health authority's IT service desk has twelve analysts. The authority is replacing its ticketing tool, and the analysts need to learn the new tool, a new way of handling requests, and basic skills in diagnosing network problems that used to be handled by another team. The first plan was a three-day course for everyone, which would have closed the desk for most of a week.

The desk manager takes another route. She names a senior analyst as coach and asks each analyst for a personal goal linked to the change, such as mastering the new request catalog or learning to read basic network logs. The team runs three-week learning cycles. In each, pairs work on real tickets in the new tool with a more experienced colleague, and at the end each pair shows the team one thing they learned and one thing that confused them. A test copy of the new tool allows practice without risk to live tickets. The authority's mandatory security training stays as a formal course with recorded completion.

Three cycles later, most analysts handle common requests in the new tool without help, and the senior analyst has started a shared page of tips that new colleagues now use. The cycle also shows where learning is hard. Two analysts who work night shifts rarely overlap with the coach, and one analyst feels uncomfortable showing confusion in front of the team. The manager arranges time for the night analysts to pair with the coach by video, and agrees with the team that confusion shown in the demonstrations is a sign of useful learning, not weakness. She also notes that the network skills are still thin and adds a longer cycle to build them.

## Four perspectives

### Product and delivery teams

Delivery teams already have most of the machinery for agile learning: short cycles, demonstrations, and retrospectives. Add learning goals to your backlog, so that a new technology or skill competes openly with features. Share what you learn with operations through demonstrations and written notes.

### Service and operations teams

Operations teams learn heavily from incidents, and from pairing during on-call. Make time for deliberate practice, such as game days in a test environment, and record what you learn so that the next shift can use it. Protect required training and use agile cycles to apply it.

### Leadership and governance

Leaders decide whether learning has time and money. Ask what skills the organization will need in a year, and fund learning as you would other investments. Keep records of required training. Resist measuring learning only by hours or course counts, which tells you little about capability.

### Customers and users

Customers benefit when service staff keep learning, though they rarely see it directly. They see better answers, fewer escalations, and quicker resolution. Where you can, share feedback on what staff do well and what confuses you, since it guides what the team learns next.

## Common failure modes

- **Treating learning as spare-time activity.** Delivery always wins, and skills decay. Instead, reserve a regular share of time and protect it.
- **Replacing required training with self-directed learning.** Gaps appear in safety, security, or compliance. Instead, keep defined training where completeness matters and use agile learning to supplement it.
- **No coach or sponsor.** Nobody sets priorities or helps people reflect. Instead, name both roles and give them time.
- **Learning goals unconnected to work.** People attend courses that never get used. Instead, tie each goal to a real task in the coming weeks.
- **Measuring attendance.** Completion counts replace evidence of capability. Instead, watch signals from the work, such as onboarding time and escalations.
- **Learning stays private.** Knowledge sits with individuals. Instead, end each cycle with a demonstration or note that others can use.
- **Blame when practice goes wrong.** People stop trying. Instead, provide safe places to fail and respond to mistakes with questions.
- **Over-relying on evidence that is thin.** A team adopts the method wholesale because an article recommends it. Instead, treat it as an experiment and review results.

## Maturity model

| Dimension | Initiate | Develop | Standardize | Manage | Orchestrate |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Planning learning | Learning is ad hoc and left to individuals | Some teams set learning goals | Teams plan learning in short cycles with goals linked to work | Learning goals are reviewed against capability and results | Learning priorities are coordinated across teams and with partners |
| Support and roles | Nobody is responsible for learning | Some teams have an informal coach | Each team has a sponsor and a coach with time to act | Coaching and sponsor effectiveness are reviewed | Coaches and communities of practice support learning across teams |
| Sharing knowledge | Knowledge stays with individuals | Some teams write notes or demonstrate | Cycles end with a shared demonstration or record | Use of shared knowledge is observed and gaps are tracked | Knowledge flows freely between teams and into runbooks and training |
| Required training | Required training is inconsistent | Courses exist, with patchy records | Required training is defined, tracked, and complete | Training effectiveness is reviewed against incidents and audits | Required training is aligned across teams, suppliers, and partners |

## Checklist

- [ ] The team has named a sponsor for learning priorities and a coach.
- [ ] Each person has at least one personal learning goal linked to team needs.
- [ ] A regular share of working time is reserved for learning.
- [ ] Learning is done in short cycles, with a visible result at the end of each.
- [ ] A safe place exists to practice, such as a test environment or game day.
- [ ] Required training for safety, security, and regulation is defined, tracked, and completed.
- [ ] Lessons from incidents are recorded and shared beyond the team.
- [ ] Skills held by only one person have been identified and have a plan.
- [ ] We have chosen a work-based signal to see whether learning is paying off.
- [ ] Learning goals are reviewed at least once a quarter.

## AI prompts

**Purpose: map the skills your team needs and where it has gaps.**

```text
Help me map the skills our team needs and where we have gaps.

The services we run and build: [describe in a few sentences].
Skills we use now, with how many people can do each: [list, such as "incident triage: 6 people; database failover: 1 person"].
Changes coming in the next year: [list tools, services, or regulations].

Produce a table of skills with columns: skill, people who can do it, importance to the service (as I described it), and risk (low, medium, high, with a one-sentence reason). Highlight skills held by one person. Suggest one low-risk way to build each high-risk skill. Do not invent skills or numbers beyond what I gave. Ask clarifying questions first, mark assumptions, and list what you are unsure of.
```

**Purpose: plan a three-week learning cycle for a team.**

```text
Plan a three-week learning cycle for a team of [number] people who need to learn [skill or tool].

Why it matters now: [one or two sentences].
Real work the team can practice on: [describe, or write "unknown"].
Time available per person per week: [hours].
Constraints: [for example shifts, remote work, no test environment].

Give a goal for the cycle, a plan by week, how pairs or groups will practice on real tasks, a way for people to show what they learned at the end, and two questions for a short review. Keep it free of any particular tool or course. Ask clarifying questions first if information is missing, mark assumptions, and list what you are unsure of.
```

**Purpose: turn an incident into a learning note for others.**

```text
Help me write a one-page learning note from an incident.

Facts of the incident, in order, blameless: [paste].
What we did to restore service: [paste].
What we changed afterwards: [paste, or write "nothing yet"].

Write a note with: what happened in plain words, what we learned (separating what is known from what is a guess), what others should do or check, and open questions. Use only the facts I gave. Do not invent causes. Ask clarifying questions first if the facts are too thin, and list what you are unsure of.
```

**Purpose: decide which learning needs formal training and which can be learned on the job.**

```text
Here is a list of things our team needs to learn this year: [paste a list].

Rules we must follow for training, with the source text where you have it (for example internal policy or regulation): [paste, or write "none known"].

For each item, suggest whether it needs formal, defined training with a completion record, or can be learned through work, pairing, or experiments, and give a one-sentence reason. Treat anything related to safety, security, or compliance with caution and say so. Do not claim that any rule requires training unless I pasted its text. Mark assumptions, and list what you are unsure of.
```
